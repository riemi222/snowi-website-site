First Time Skiing in Hokkaido: How Do I Get from New Chitose Airport to the Resort? Do I Need a Private Transfer?

For a first Hokkaido ski trip, the most common way from New Chitose Airport to the slopes is a resort shuttle bus (like Resort Liner) or a pre-booked private transfer: Niseko takes about 2.5-3 hours, Rusutsu about 2-2.5 hours, and Sapporo Teine only 1-1.5 hours once you connect through Sapporo. If you're travelling with bulky ski gear, lots of luggage, or kids, a door-to-door private transfer is worth arranging so you're not hauling bags between buses. SNOWI helps match your flight schedule and accommodation to the right New Chitose Airport transfer option so your first day in Hokkaido starts smoothly.
Bus, JR, or Private Transfer — Which Should I Choose?

Resort buses to Niseko run mainly through Hokkaido Resort Liner, White Liner, and Chuo Bus, with Resort Liner offering the most frequent departures; a one-way adult ticket runs about JPY 5,000 (children aged 3-11 about JPY 4,000), with a rest stop partway through the journey. Buses heading to Niseko usually stop at Rusutsu first, so the ride to Rusutsu tends to be a bit shorter. Sapporo Teine sits close to central Sapporo, so it's often easier to take the JR or airport bus into Sapporo first (as fast as 33 minutes, from about JPY 1,230) and transfer by local bus or taxi from there, rather than waiting for a direct resort bus. For groups of eight or more, or if you're carrying a lot of gear, a private door-to-door transfer costs more than the bus but is usually cheaper than several taxis, and you don't risk the bus selling out.

How Much Time Should I Leave for Check-In at New Chitose Airport?
  • Landing at the domestic terminal, meeting point in the domestic terminal: allow at least 45 minutes
  • Landing at the international terminal, meeting point in the international terminal: allow at least 90 minutes
  • Landing at the international terminal, meeting point in the domestic terminal: allow at least 120 minutes

If you're arriving on an international flight, immigration and baggage claim often take longer than expected, so cutting it close on the meeting time is a common way to miss a pre-booked bus or transfer.

Travelling with Kids or a Lot of Gear — What's the Best Option?

If you're travelling with young children or carrying skis, boards, and other bulky gear, a private door-to-door transfer saves a lot of hassle compared to hauling everything through a bus terminal — the driver takes you straight from the airport to your hotel or resort entrance. During peak season (late December through February), bus seats can sell out more than a week in advance, so it's worth locking in your transfer option 2-4 weeks ahead, and even earlier around New Year's or Lunar New Year.

Should I Rent a Car and Self-Drive for Hokkaido Skiing? Is Winter Driving Safe?

If you have no winter driving experience, self-driving during Hokkaido's December-to-March ski season isn't recommended — taking the JR, buses, or a transfer service is safer. Roads can turn dangerous from "black ice," and visibility can drop to just a few meters in a whiteout, conditions even locals find challenging. When planning a Hokkaido ski trip, SNOWI prioritizes helping first-time winter drivers arrange bus or private transfer alternatives instead, so a first ski trip doesn't also become a crash course in winter driving.
What Are the Dangers of Winter Driving? What Is Black Ice?

From December through March, Hokkaido's roads are commonly covered in snow and ice, and drivers without winter experience can easily lose control on a slippery surface. The most dangerous hazard is "black ice" — a road surface that looks merely wet but is actually frozen solid, often found near tunnel entrances and bridges; tires grip better below about 25 km/h, so it's worth slowing down cautiously through any suspicious-looking wet patch. During a whiteout (snowstorm), visibility can plunge to just a few meters, and heavy snow can bury road signs while GPS navigation becomes unreliable — a real challenge even for experienced local drivers.

What If I Have No Winter Driving Experience — How Should I Plan Transport?
  • During the December-to-March peak season, drivers without winter experience should skip the rental car and use JR, buses, or a day-tour transfer instead
  • If you must self-drive, keep daily distances to roughly 100-200 km, finish driving by 3:30pm, and arrive at your accommodation by 5pm to avoid night driving
  • Check real-time road condition services like Hokkaido no Michi Navi and blizzard visibility reports before setting out, and be ready to cancel the day's plan if heavy snow or a whiteout is forecast
If I Do Have to Self-Drive, What Are Some Practical Tips?

Rent a 4WD vehicle fitted with winter tires. Ease onto the accelerator when starting to avoid spinning the wheels, and if you feel the car slide, ease off the gas immediately and wait for traction to return before accelerating again. Avoid hard braking — use light, early pumps instead — and keep at least double the normal following distance, since stopping distances on snow are much longer than usual. Slow down well before any turn and steer gently; rural roads and mountain passes often have deer or foxes dart out, especially at dawn and dusk, so slow down for animal-crossing signs. Even for a minor scrape, report it to the police (110) before contacting the rental company — without a police report, insurance won't pay out no matter how comprehensive the policy.

What Age Can Kids Start Ski Lessons? How Do I Choose a Chinese-Speaking Instructor for Family Ski Lessons?

The industry consensus is that 3-5 years old is a reasonable age to introduce kids to skiing, with most ski schools recommending a formal beginner class starting around age 4-5; younger children or first-timers on snow are usually better suited to one-on-one lessons. Hokkaido's soft powder snow makes falls less painful for little ones, and having a Chinese-speaking instructor makes it much easier for parents to follow their child's progress. SNOWI matches families with the right family ski lesson and Chinese-speaking instructor based on the child's age and experience.
What Do Kids' Ski Lessons Cover? Is It the Same as an Adult Beginner Class?

The core concepts in kids' lessons are similar to an adult beginner class, just taught more playfully: using the "Pizza" and "French Fries" foot-shape analogy to teach wedging and parallel positions for controlling speed, which is every child's first step into skiing; from there, lessons move into balance, simple turns, and — most importantly — how to fall and get back up safely, which reduces injury risk while building confidence. A single lesson usually won't cover both skiing and snowboarding at once, since the two sports use different techniques and equipment, so it's best to focus on one discipline first.

Under What Age Should Lessons Be One-on-One? Which Resorts Are Best for Families?

Some ski schools' children's safety guidelines specifically state that beginner children under 7 can only take one-on-one lessons, not group classes, so the instructor can watch the child's technique and safety closely throughout. For resort choice, Furano is a common recommendation for a family's first ski trip, with a kids' teaching zone and moving walkway at the base area; Tomamu suits families wanting to combine skiing with a resort stay; and Rusutsu works well for families where the adults are more advanced skiers. Many Hokkaido resorts offer free lift passes for children under 12, though some start charging from age 4 or 6, so it's worth confirming the exact policy before booking.

How Far in Advance Should I Book a Family Ski Lesson?

Family lessons work the same as adult private lessons — first come, first served — and popular Chinese-speaking instructors and time slots fill up especially fast around Christmas, New Year's, and Lunar New Year. It's worth booking at least one month ahead, and 2-3 months ahead if your trip falls during one of those holiday periods, so you're not scrambling to find a suitable Chinese-speaking instructor at the last minute.

Do I need a Chinese-speaking ski instructor in Hokkaido? How much does it cost?

If it's your first time skiing, or your trip is short, we recommend booking at least one lesson with a Chinese-speaking instructor — it saves a lot of time on balance and stopping technique. A full-day Chinese-speaking instructor in Hokkaido typically costs around NT$15,000-22,000, depending on instructor level, resort location and group size, and usually covers instruction only — lift tickets, equipment rental and insurance are separate.
How is a Chinese-speaking instructor different from a Japanese-speaking one?

Resorts with Chinese-speaking instructors or ski schools in Hokkaido are mainly concentrated in Niseko, Rusutsu, Kiroro and Sapporo Teine. The biggest advantage of a Chinese-speaking instructor is being able to communicate technique and safety cues directly in your native language instead of relying on body language — especially useful for complete beginners or short trips.

How much do Chinese-speaking lessons cost in Rusutsu and Niseko?
  • Rusutsu: Officially certified Chinese-speaking teaching resources are available at Rusutsu Resort, with native Chinese-speaking instructors holding international certifications from Japan, New Zealand and other countries. Full-day lessons typically run around JPY 15,000 per day (including instructor transport and lift ticket).
  • Niseko: A weekday 3-hour lesson (1-4 people) runs about JPY 38,000 total, dropping to about JPY 9,500 per person when split among 4 skiers. Half-day (3-hour) and full-day (6-hour, including 1-hour break) options are common, plus evening lessons after 17:00.
  • A 6-hour full-day private lesson in Rusutsu (Chinese/Cantonese/English) typically runs JPY 70,000-76,000.
Is a private lesson or a group lesson better value?

Group lessons are cheaper and let you meet other skiers, which suits complete beginners who want to progress step by step — most instructors take up to 4 students, and the fee is split among the group. Private lessons progress faster since the instructor focuses entirely on your technique, which suits nervous beginners, short trips, or advanced skiers who want to work on specific skills.

Hokkaido ski lessons generally split into beginner classes and advanced classes. Beginner classes build balance, the snowplow stop and basic turns from scratch, usually as group lessons or short private sessions. Advanced classes are for skiers who already have a foundation, focusing on S-turns, edge control and powder runs, typically delivered one-on-one or in small groups tailored to the individual's level.

Ski or Snowboard for Your First Time? How Beginners Should Decide

If it's your first time and your trip is short, start with skis - you face forward with independent control over each ski, the movement feels more natural, you'll likely fall less, and it's easier to walk around and queue for lifts. If you have more days and want to try the sideways-facing thrill of riding, snowboarding is worth considering. When SNOWI plans lessons for first-timers, we ask about your experience level and trip length before recommending which to try and which instructor to pair you with.
Why is skiing usually easier for first-timers?

With skis, each foot controls its own ski and you face forward, which is closer to the natural logic of walking and standing - so beginners tend to fall less often early on. Skis are also easier to walk around in off the slopes, like at lift lines or on flat ground, which matters a lot if you're new to skiing and only have a day or two on your trip, making the learning curve noticeably gentler.

Is snowboarding harder to learn? What should you watch out for?

With snowboarding, both feet are strapped to a single board, and you steer by shifting your body weight and engaging your core, so it takes more balance practice early on and you'll usually fall more than you would on skis. That said, once you get a feel for the sideways momentum, progress can actually speed up, and it opens the door to freestyle tricks more easily - it's a better fit if your trip is longer and you're willing to put in the time.

If you're bringing kids, should they learn skiing or snowboarding?

Kids aged 3 and up are generally recommended to start with skiing, since the movements are simpler and they'll fall less, which keeps things safer and more encouraging for young beginners. Snowboarding is usually better saved for age 7 and up, once a child's core strength and balance have developed more.

  • Skiing: face forward, independent leg control, easy to walk in, fewer falls - good for first-timers and short trips
  • Snowboarding: both feet on one board, needs more balance and core strength, can progress fast once you get it, good for longer trips and freestyle
  • Kids: skiing recommended from age 3+, snowboarding better from age 7+

How Should Beginners Dress for Skiing in Hokkaido? A Complete Layering Guide

Temperatures during Hokkaido's ski season (December to February) typically range from -12°C to -3°C, and can drop below -20°C in mountain areas. First-time skiers in Hokkaido should use a three-layer system: a moisture-wicking base layer, an insulating mid layer, and a windproof, waterproof outer layer, to stay warm without sacrificing mobility. Before every first lesson, SNOWI reminds students to prepare their clothing following this principle so gear issues never get in the way of learning.
What goes in each of the three layers?

The base layer should be moisture-wicking thermal underwear or wool material that keeps skin dry and prevents heat loss from sweat. The mid layer — a fleece jacket, wool sweater, or lightweight down jacket — provides the main insulation. The outer layer must be a waterproof, windproof, breathable long ski jacket paired with ski pants to truly block Hokkaido's wind, snow and moisture. The advantage of layering is that you can add or remove pieces depending on activity level and indoor/outdoor temperature swings — for example, when entering a heated restaurant or gondola station, you only need to remove the outer layer instead of changing your entire outfit.

  • Base layer: moisture-wicking thermal underwear, wool base layers
  • Mid layer: fleece jacket, wool sweater, lightweight down jacket
  • Outer layer: waterproof, windproof long ski jacket + ski pants
Should I bring my own gloves, socks and protective gear?

Bring your own cold-weather gloves, knee-high wool ski socks, a warm hat and a scarf, since most rental shops don't rent out these personal items for hygiene reasons. If you're worried about falls, wrist guards and knee pads are also worth packing yourself — first-time skiers who are still learning balance and how to snowplow-stop tend to fall more often, so extra protection lowers the risk of wrist and knee injuries.

Why does the big indoor-outdoor temperature gap in Hokkaido matter for what I wear?

Indoor spaces in Hokkaido — malls, restaurants, gondola stations — are usually heated to around 20-24°C, more than 20 degrees warmer than outside. Wearing one thick single-layer coat makes it easy to overheat and sweat every time you move indoors, which actually raises your risk of catching a chill. That's exactly why a layering system works better than a single heavy coat for a Hokkaido ski trip — it lets you adjust on the fly.

How to Choose Ski Gloves: Are Mittens Warmer Than 5-Finger Gloves for Beginners?

For a first ski trip, start with 5-finger cloth gloves — they offer better dexterity for gripping poles and adjusting gear, and cost less than technical mittens. If your hands run cold easily, or you know you'll be skiing on an especially frigid day, switch to mittens for extra warmth; SNOWI's pre-ski lesson briefing also flags which glove type suits the day's forecast.
What's the actual difference between 5-finger gloves and mittens?

Five-finger gloves wrap each finger separately, which means more surface area exposed to cold air and somewhat less warmth, but they give you precise control for gripping ski poles, adjusting goggles, or working a zipper — anything that needs individual finger movement. Mittens keep the thumb separate but bundle the other four fingers together in one shared space; because your fingers share body heat and there's less surface area exposed to cold air, mittens are generally warmer, though the trade-off is reduced dexterity for gripping poles or handling gear. Lobster gloves split the difference, freeing up the index finger while bundling the rest — a middle-ground option between warmth and control.

  • 5-finger gloves: less warm, best dexterity and precision, generally more affordable
  • Mittens: warmer, but less dexterity for gripping poles or gear
  • Lobster gloves: index finger free, rest bundled — a middle-ground option
Which type should first-time skiers choose?

If you're only buying one pair to cover most conditions in a beginner ski lesson, five-finger cloth gloves tend to be the more practical choice — affordable and flexible enough for the frequent hand movements involved in gripping poles and pushing yourself up after a fall. If you know your hands run cold, or you're heading out on a particularly cold, windy day, pack a pair of mittens as a backup — some skiers even layer a thin glove liner underneath mittens for extra warmth without fully losing dexterity.

Beyond warmth, what else matters when choosing ski gloves?

Waterproofing, wind resistance, and abrasion resistance matter just as much as warmth — beginners tend to fall more often, so gloves need durable, water-resistant material to protect your hands from scraping against the snow and to avoid getting soaked through early in the day. Try gloves on in person before buying to confirm there's enough room to move your fingers and that the cuffs seal properly against your jacket sleeves, keeping wind and snow from sneaking in throughout the ski lesson.

How is Japan's ski instructor certification system structured — what's the difference between SAJ, SBJ, and SIA?

Japan has three main ski-instructor-related organizations: SAJ (mainly alpine skiing, also covering snowboarding), SBJ/JSBA (snowboard-only), and SIA (a pure ski instructor association). Take SAJ's technical grading as an example: it runs from Level 1 to Level 5. Level 5 is roughly the ability to do a controlled snowplow or J-turn forward and backward; Levels 3-4 require smooth linked S-turns on a gentle slope; Levels 1-2 demand much more advanced technique. Passing the Level 2 exam earns an ID-card-sized license, and Level 1 earns an award-style certificate. Going further to become an actual "instructor" (指導員) requires passing the technical grading first, then waiting a full year before you're even eligible to sit the instructor exam — the whole process is far from quick.

  • SAJ: alpine-focused technical grading and instructor system, Levels 1 through 5
  • SBJ/JSBA: snowboard-specific grading system
  • SIA: a dedicated ski instructor association focused on teaching qualifications
Why do so many Chinese-speaking instructors hold Canadian CSIA/CASI certification rather than Japan's SAJ?

Japan's official instructor certification comes with a Japanese-language barrier, plus a drawn-out process — the required grading exam followed by a mandatory waiting period before the instructor exam. As a result, relatively few Taiwanese or Hong Kong instructors hold official Japanese credentials. By comparison, Canada's CSIA (alpine) and CASI (snowboard) certifications have a more complete teaching curriculum and stronger international recognition, making them the most common licenses held by Chinese-speaking instructors today. We recommend looking for instructors certified at Level 2 or above under an Interski member body such as CSIA/CASI, NZSIA, or APSI — that tends to correlate with more consistent teaching quality.

How can I confirm a ski school is officially certified by the resort?

Scared of Falling on Your First Ski Trip? Beginner Mindset Tips and How to Fall Correctly

Feeling nervous and scared of falling the first time you stand on skis is completely normal — the goal isn't to avoid falling altogether, it's learning to fall safely: don't brace with your hands, don't flail your limbs, and don't roll your body. Will I definitely fall a lot on my first day? How do I mentally prepare?

Yes — falling on your first day is an unavoidable part of the process. Almost every beginner goes through repeated "landing on your backside" moments, and it has little to do with athletic ability; it's simply your body adjusting to skis, boots, and a completely different center of gravity and friction than you're used to. Rather than rushing to keep pace with friends, it helps to set a lower bar for day one — focus on feeling the snow and the freedom of gliding, not on proving how good you are. A relaxed mindset actually helps your muscles stay loose, which reduces the chance of a harder fall from being tense.

  • How often you fall has little to do with athletic ability — it's a normal part of adjusting to a new center of gravity
  • Set day-one goals around building balance and feeling the glide, not speed
  • A relaxed mindset reduces muscle tension, which in turn lowers the risk of a harder fall
What's the correct way to fall to reduce injury risk?

Instructors typically start a lesson by teaching beginners how to "fall safely." Remember the three don'ts when falling: don't brace with your hands (risks wrist injury), don't flail your arms and legs (increases sprain risk), and don't deliberately roll your body (risks getting tangled in skis and poles). The correct technique is to fall in the direction you're already moving, letting your body land on its side or sit down, relaxing your muscles so your gear absorbs the impact naturally. Most beginner lessons take place on slopes under 5 degrees — often an even gentler "bunny slope" — letting first-timers repeatedly practice falling and getting back up in a low-risk environment before progressing to a real beginner run.

Beyond falling technique, what else can help ease nervousness?

If you suddenly feel nervous mid-run, pull over to the side and take a few deep breaths before continuing — there's no need to force yourself through an entire run in one go. Choosing gentle, wide-open beginner runs (usually marked as green) also noticeably reduces anxiety. It also helps to do simple squats and lunges one to two weeks before your trip, so your legs get used to the posture skiing requires — this reduces the instability that comes from both nervousness and fatigue.

Is It Normal to Be Sore All Over After Skiing? How Long Do Beginners Take to Recover?

Being sore all over after skiing is normal — with enough rest, protein, and stretching, most beginners feel better within a few days. That's just muscle repairing and getting stronger, not an injury. Why does skiing make you so sore? What's actually happening?

Skiing works muscle groups most people rarely train — especially the front of the thighs, glutes, and core, which stay under constant load during the wedge stop and low-stance balancing. When a beginner does this for hours straight for the first time, the muscle fibers develop tiny tears, known as delayed-onset muscle soreness (DOMS). That's a normal response to unfamiliar, high-intensity effort, not a sign of injury. Add in a full day of falling and pushing back up with your wrists, and sore wrists and shoulders are common too.

  • Front thighs, glutes, core: sore from sustained wedge-stop braking and low-stance balance
  • Wrists, shoulders: sore from repeatedly pushing up off the snow after falls
  • This soreness is muscle repairing itself — not an injury
How long until the soreness goes away? Anything that speeds it up?

With enough rest, adequate protein, and stretching, massage, or foam rolling, soreness usually clears within a few days — and the muscle comes back stronger through that repair process, which is part of why day two or three of a ski trip often feels smoother than day one. What kind of soreness means it might actually be an injury?

If sharp pain doesn't ease after rest and lasts beyond a week — especially with joint swelling or limited range of motion — that's worth taking seriously. A 5-10 minute dynamic warm-up before skiing (squats, lunges, joint circles) also measurably reduces both soreness and injury risk.
